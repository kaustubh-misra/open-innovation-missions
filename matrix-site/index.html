Open innovation programs: where they succeeded and failed

Open science components mostly succeed. Failures cluster in infrastructure: maintenance, funding continuity and moving from discovery to market. NGO-led projects are often strong at building open tools and data, and weakest at local ownership, scaling through government and staying funded.

How to read this matrix
Columns and rows
Each column is a program. Each row is a layer of openness: open science, open hardware and making, and open tech and infrastructure. A cell lists the program's components in that layer.
Layer qualifiers
Under each layer are the principles a component must broadly meet to count as open in that layer, drawn from the linked definitions. Some programs meet them only in part: the Fab Charter lets inventors protect designs, and TechShop offered open access to tools but not open designs.
Who leads, and the NGO test
Each program is tagged by who leads it. A program counts as NGO-led when a nonprofit controls governance and stewardship of the open outputs, plus at least one of agenda, funding or delivery. Academic partners may still take part. MOSIP is included as a contrast: philanthropy-funded, but hosted and governed by an academic institute. Several NGO-led entries are single organisations rather than coalitions; each column header says how it meets the test.
Colours
Green and red mark broad success and failure, judged from each program's own reports or from external critiques. Amber is added for components where the evidence is mixed, contested or not yet in, rather than forcing them into green or red.
Evidence type
Self-reported means the program, its institution or a commissioned evaluator wrote it. External means peer-reviewed research, government agencies outside the program, or journalism.
What is left out
This is a selection from the programs discussed, not a census. Many programs have no published failure analysis, so the record leans toward success.
